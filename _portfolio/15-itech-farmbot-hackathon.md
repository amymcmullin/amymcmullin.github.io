*Immokalee Technical College · November 21–22, 2016 · Funded by a Champions for Learning classroom grant*

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/gAdBdvalqWA" title="iTech Hackathon 2016" frameborder="0" allowfullscreen></iframe>

## The idea

[FarmBot](https://farm.bot/) is an open-source CNC farming machine — precision agriculture at garden scale, driven by a Raspberry Pi and Arduino boards. I wanted one in the iTech courtyard, built by students.

The interesting constraint was that no single program could build it. The machine needs precision-machined parts, 3D-printed components, a raised bed, a solar and rainwater system, and someone who actually knows what to plant. That's five trades. So instead of running it as a Computer Systems project, I scoped it as a campus project and split the build:

| Program | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Computer Systems | 3D-print components; server technology and electronics |
| Machining | CNC-manufacture precision parts |
| Construction | Design and build the raised farm bed |
| Automotive | Design the solar and rainwater collection systems |
| Culinary Arts | Plan the garden and select crops |

Over 150 students across those five programs were directly involved, on a campus of about 350.

## Why a hackathon

The hard part of a cross-program project isn't the engineering — it's that students in five separate trades have no idea what the others contribute, and a year-long build gives them no reason to find out. A kickoff event solves that in two days.

I ran it as a hackathon: small mixed groups, parallel problem-solving, presentations at the end. Over the two days, groups rotated through:

- Choosing a site and performing a solar study
- Designing the farm bed and selecting crops
- Setting up the Raspberry Pi and Arduino boards that drive the robotics
- Building a project budget
- 3D-printing a FarmBot component

Monday opened with everyone together — what a FarmBot is, why we were building one, then team-building before the first block left. Tuesday afternoon, the groups built and delivered presentations of their work to each other.

## The scheduling problem

This is the part I'd want to talk about in an interview, because it's where the real design work was.

Technical college students don't share a timetable. Dual-enrollment students arrive and leave on their own blocks, so at any given hour I had a different population in the room. And the activities were equipment-bound — one 3D printer, one set of Arduino boards, a limited number of stations.

So the event ran as a staggered round-robin rather than a schedule. Groups rotated through stations in an order that kept every piece of equipment occupied while absorbing students arriving and departing mid-event. Partial participation was designed in from the start, not accommodated after the fact.

That structure is the thing I reuse most. The AI Innovation Lab I'm building now is organized exactly the same way — equipment as stations, teams rotating through — because it's the only format that survives a real CTE timetable.

## What came of it

The hackathon launched the build. Through the fall semester each program produced its parts; in spring the trades assembled the machine and Computer Systems brought up the electronics and server side.

It also turned out to be a recruiting event I hadn't planned. The district guidance office asked to bring prospective students to watch the opening and the final presentations — which says something about what two days of students visibly building a farming robot does for a program's story.
